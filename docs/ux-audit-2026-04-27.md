## CAPS UX BEHAVIORAL AUDIT — v2.7.0 EAS 328
**Date:** Apr 27 2026 | **Lens:** Senior Product Designer / Behavioral Design

---

### Section 1 — Intent map

The Home screen (app/(tabs)/index.tsx) has one declared intent: get the player into a game. Everything on the screen — the felt texture, the chip display, the "Play" button — exists to execute that single CTA. But the screen accumulates secondary intents as features accrue, and those intents are now in active tension with the primary one. The daily reward popup fires on mount when claimable, the streak badge pill sits in the header, a nudge modal for Google sign-in can appear, the activity feed and recent hands block are surfaced for veteran players, and a "Try 4 boards" upgrade nudge can surface on the results screen immediately after the first game. None of these are wrong individually. Collectively they dilute the one intent the screen was built around.

The Game screen has a clear and singular behavioral intent: the player must place 16 cards across 4 boards (or 12 across 3) before the 30-second timer expires. Every element on this screen that is not directly related to that task is accidental friction. The floatingActions bar (place/undo) is primary signal — it is what the player interacts with. The topBar chip display is secondary context. The bot status section and countdown label are tertiary orientation cues. But as Section 2 details, the visual hierarchy does not reflect this ranking.

The Results screen has a fractured intent. On the surface it presents outcome data: win/loss, boards won, chip delta, hand names. But it is simultaneously running a conversion funnel (LoginPromptModal for anonymous users on games 3–20), a celebration system (win overlay, chip shower, complete overlay), a coaching loop (efficiency hint, EfficiencyCard), and a re-engagement hook (auto-continue countdown starting at 1.5 seconds). These intents are not compatible within a single screen moment. The celebration needs the user to pause and feel something. The LoginPromptModal interrupts that feeling with a conversion ask. The auto-continue countdown races against both. The result is a screen that executes all of its intents poorly instead of one of them well.

The Settings screen is coherent: it exists to let the player configure things. The friction is deliberate where account deletion requires confirmation steps. No conflicting intent.

### Section 2 — Hierarchy collapse on game screen

The game screen's font size table and zIndex stack reveal a hierarchy that was built feature-by-feature rather than designed top-down. The primary task — place 16 cards — is not represented by any text on screen. It is implicit. The visual hierarchy instead surfaces elements in this order by size:

| Element | Current weight | Should be | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watermark (fiveoWatermarkText) | rf(52), zIndex 1 | Hidden / removed | Accidental. Was a debug artifact. Style still present in stylesheet at line 1218 even after removal from render. |
| Float action buttons (place/undo) | rf(16), zIndex 10 | rf(16)–rf(18), zIndex 20 | These are the PRIMARY interaction affordances — they should sit above board content visually |
| Bot status text | rf(14), zIndex 10 | rf(11) | The bot is secondary context, not primary action. It shares zIndex with the topBar. |
| Header chips amount | rf(14), zIndex 10 | rf(12) | Chip balance during play is ambient info, not decision-critical |
| Countdown timer label | rf(10) | rf(10) + color pulse | Correct size but needs motion cue — static label at rf(10) is not noticed under cognitive load |
| Board labels (לוח 1/2) | rf(10) | rf(10) | Correct |
| Board error text | rf(12) | rf(12) | Correct |
| Calculating text | rf(14) | rf(12) | Appears post-placement, not needed at primary size |
| Auto-place toast | zIndex 999 | zIndex 999 | Correct — this is a system message, should occlude everything |

The zIndex stack tells the clearest story. At 999 sits the auto-place toast — correct, it is an interruptive system message. At 99 sits the auto-place flash overlay. At 40 sits the tooltip overlay. At 10 sits the topBar and floatingActions simultaneously. Two things share zIndex 10: the meta-information bar (chip balance, back button) and the primary interactive affordances (place/undo). This means the interface treats "how many chips I have" and "the button I use to play the game" as equal priority. They are not. The behavioral implication is that players who are new to the game cannot orient to what matters. Their eyes have no visual anchor that says "this is what you do next." The largest text visible on the game screen (after the watermark style is removed but before the watermark text is conditionally hidden) has no connection to any action.

### Section 3 — Feedback gaps

The first gap is in the reveal flow. When the game transitions from game.tsx to results.tsx, the player sees a board-by-board reveal via BoardReveal, then lands on results.tsx showing a win/loss score. What is absent is any explanation of *why* a board was won or lost at the hand level. The results screen surfaces `b.playerHandName` (e.g. "Two Pair") and the opponent's hand name, but does not surface the community cards that made those hands. The player sees "YOU LOSE — 1–3" but cannot learn which of their placements caused which losses. The feedback needed to improve does not exist at the moment when the player is most receptive to it. This gap lives in results.tsx around lines 720–721 where `playerHandName` is displayed, and in BoardResultCard which receives the board data but does not render the community board.

The second gap is the loss explanation itself. The efficiency hint at the bottom of the results screen (results.tsx line 481, via `getEfficiencyHint`) does run a card-swap calculation and can surface something like "Moving 9♥ from Board 1 to Board 2 would improve by 22%". But this hint only fires when `bestImprovement > 0` (results.tsx line 71), it only reflects a single best swap, and it is positioned far down the scroll — below the board results, below the XP bar, below the share section. A player who auto-continues in the 7-second window (AUTO_CONTINUE_SECS) never sees it. The hint that exists is correct; its placement ensures it is almost never read.

The third gap is the "why did I lose this board" moment. Each board shows a winner label. There is no inline explanation of the winning hand, no comparison view, no breakdown of how the Omaha hand evaluator selected the best two cards from each player's four. This is the most important teaching moment in the game — the 2-of-4 rule is the game's core mechanic and the thing that surprises new players most — and results.tsx has no UI surface for it beyond a static hand name string.

### Section 4 — Empty state graveyard

The Friends tab (app/(tabs)/friends.tsx) is a navigation menu masquerading as a social feature. It contains four cards that each route to other screens: referral, leaderboard, host game, join game. For a new player with zero friends in the app, zero multiplayer history, and zero context about what "Host Online Game" means, the screen is a dead-end disguised as an entry point. There is no empty state, no call to action, no explanation of what this tab becomes when friends are added. The screen renders identically for a first-time user and a veteran with an active friend network, which means it communicates nothing about the value it could eventually deliver. The behavioral failure here is the absence of a compelling "here is what this will look like when it's alive" state.

The Cups tab (app/(tabs)/cups.tsx) has better structure — it renders a loading state, then maps through cup items with progress bars for unearned cups. But for a new user, all cups are locked and partially opaque. The progress bar fills to `Math.min(100, cup.progress)%`, but `cup.progress` for a new user is 0 across the board. The screen presents a list of locked items with no description of what action earns each cup and no prioritization of which cup is closest to being earned. It is a trophy cabinet shown to someone who has never won a match, with no path forward surfaced on the screen itself.

The activity feed at app/(tabs)/index.tsx line 1635 has a text fallback: "שחק סיט אנד גו כדי לראות את ההיסטוריה שלך" (play sit-and-go to see your history). This is the one correct empty state in the codebase — it has a CTA embedded in the text. But it is gated behind `show_veteran` (line 1632), which means it only appears for players the app has already classified as non-new. New players never see it at all, defeating the purpose of showing it when the feed is genuinely empty.

The hand history screen (app/hand-history.tsx) is accessible from the recent hands list on Home, but for a player who has played zero games there is no entry point to it and no empty state has been confirmed to exist. The hands list on Home is gated behind `show_veteran && recentHands.length > 0` (line 1658), so new users never encounter it — but this also means the feature is invisible to any player until the app decides they are a veteran, which is an arbitrary classification based on game count.

### Section 5 — Skill-level mismatch

The app defaults `gamesPlayed` to 99 in both app/(tabs)/index.tsx (line 762) and app/game.tsx (line 165). The comment on both lines is explicit: "default 99 = not first game until loaded" and "default high so hint is hidden until loaded." The intent is a conservative fallback — assume veteran, not beginner, until AsyncStorage confirms otherwise. The side effect is that for any player whose AsyncStorage read fails, is slow, or returns null, the app treats them as having played 99 games and skips every first-time experience: the welcome screen gated at `gamesPlayed === 0` (index.tsx line 1089), the first-time hints in BoardArrangement gated at `gamesPlayed < 3` (index.tsx line 1703), and the interactive tutorial shown only if a storage key is absent (index.tsx line 942). The player who most needs onboarding — the one on a slow device whose storage reads lag — is the one most likely to be dropped into the full game without any guidance.

The interactive tutorial (index.tsx line 940) is shown based on whether the `INTERACTIVE_TUTORIAL_KEY` is absent from storage, not on game count. This means it could appear to a player who has already played 50 games on a new device. The skip button is available ("דלג על ההדרכה"), which is correct friction for a returning player, but the system cannot distinguish between a genuine first-time player and a reinstall. Both get the same tutorial offer. Meanwhile, a player who dismisses the tutorial on game 1 and then finds the game confusing on game 4 has no way to replay the tutorial without going into Settings — a path that assumes they know the tutorial exists and know where to find Settings.

The TIPS array in game.tsx (lines 55–66) introduces the 2-of-4 rule at Tip index 4. This tip only appears during a guided tutorial flow and only for players who reach Tip 5 in sequence. A player who skips the tutorial, plays 3 games, and never triggers the full guided sequence never receives the explanation of the game's core mechanic. The game's most important rule — that the app chooses your best 2 cards automatically — is gated behind a tutorial that a large fraction of players will not complete.

### Section 6 — Three anti-patterns to kill before launch

**1. Streak punishment mechanics**

The daily reward streak (index.tsx lines 961–995, via `claim_daily_streak` RPC) rewards consecutive daily logins with escalating chip rewards. The streak is displayed in the header badge pill (index.tsx line 1353). The mechanic creates a carrot-that-burns dynamic: the player is rewarded for returning, but the psychological valence of the reward inverts over time. On day 1 it feels like a bonus. By day 7 it feels like an obligation. By day 14 a missed day is experienced as a loss, not merely the absence of a gain. Loss aversion (Kahneman) means the pain of losing a 14-day streak is felt more acutely than the pleasure of a 14-day streak built up. The app is manufacturing a anxiety loop. The header badge makes the streak permanently visible, which keeps the loss-aversion pressure active every time the player opens the app. There is no streak-freeze mechanic, no grace period, and no UI that communicates what happens if the streak breaks — which means the player's imagination fills that gap with the worst-case assumption.

**2. LoginPromptModal timing vs. trust**

The login prompt is triggered in results.tsx (line 346) via `shouldPromptLogin()`, which fires for anonymous users who have played between 3 and 20 games (auth.ts line 81). The timing — immediately after game results load, on the same screen where the player is processing their win or loss — is a conversion-before-value-delivery pattern. The player has just invested effort in a game. The results screen is a moment of emotional processing, not a moment of commitment readiness. A conversion ask at this point is interrupting a feeling, not following a positive experience. The 3-day cooldown after dismissal (auth.ts line 76) means a player who dismisses it will see it again 3 days later, which trains them to dismiss it again. The prompt does not show *why* logging in is valuable — what is preserved, what cross-device benefits exist, what social features unlock. It is a bare modal asking for commitment before the player has been shown what they are committing to.

**3. Vanity metrics — chips and XP without meaningful spend**

The chip balance is displayed in the game screen topBar (headerChips, game.tsx lines 1274–1294) and throughout the home screen. XP is tracked via BattlePass (results.tsx lines 404–420). Both metrics are prominently surfaced. But chips can only be spent on match entry (and the cost is returned or redistributed within the same game), and XP gates battle pass tiers that are not yet visibly meaningful to most players. The player accumulates both numbers without a clear mental model of what they are accumulating toward. This is a closed-loop economy: the numbers go up, but the behavioral signal is the number going up, not the reward the number unlocks. Over time, a player who notices that their chip total fluctuates predictably and their XP bar fills slowly will realize the numbers are score-tracking dressed as currency. The trust damage is not from the mechanic itself but from the gap between the implied promise (you are earning something) and the realized value (you earned a number).

### Section 7 — Top 5 fixes prioritized

**Fix 1 — Surface the 2-of-4 rule at the moment of confusion, not inside a tutorial gate**

WHAT: Add a one-line inline explainer on the results screen, adjacent to each board's hand name, explaining which two player cards were selected. Something as simple as "Best combo: K♥ + 9♦" per board. WHERE: results.tsx, inside the BoardResultCard render, or in the board loop starting around line 720. WHY: The 2-of-4 rule is the game's defining mechanic and the most common source of "I don't understand how I lost" confusion. Surfacing it post-game, at the moment the player is looking at the result, creates a genuine learning loop. Without it, losing feels arbitrary. EFFORT: M (requires passing selected card data through to results — BoardReveal already computes the best combo in calculateHandResultsMulti). IMPACT: Reduces churn from confusion. The single most likely cause of a new player deleting the app is not understanding why they lost.

**Fix 2 — Replace LoginPromptModal timing with a post-celebration trigger**

WHAT: Delay the `setShowLoginPrompt(true)` call until after the win overlay has completed (3 seconds for wins) or after the user has scrolled past the board results (for losses). For wins, wire it to the `showWinOverlay` dismiss. For losses, wire it to a scroll threshold. WHERE: results.tsx line 346 — change from immediate fire to a delayed/event-triggered fire. WHY: A conversion ask presented at peak positive emotion (just won) or after the player has had a moment to process (just lost + scrolled) converts better and damages trust less than an interrupt. The user is being asked to do something; they should be in a receptive state. EFFORT: S (add a setTimeout or effect dependency on win overlay visibility). IMPACT: Improves conversion rate on the login prompt and reduces the "cold interrupt" dismissal pattern.

**Fix 3 — Add an empty state CTA to the Friends tab**

WHAT: Replace the current navigation-card layout in friends.tsx with a version that shows an empty state for users with no multiplayer history. The empty state should have a headline ("CAPS with friends is more fun"), a description of what the tab becomes when active, and a primary CTA to share the referral link. The navigation cards can remain below the empty state or collapse until the user has social activity. WHERE: app/(tabs)/friends.tsx, the entire screen is 46 lines and can be rewritten in the same file. WHY: A tab that looks the same for a new user and a social user communicates nothing about the value of returning. A well-designed empty state for the Friends tab is a growth mechanic, not just UX polish. EFFORT: S (small file, self-contained). IMPACT: Increases referral link shares by making the referral path the default action for new users instead of one of four equal options.

**Fix 4 — Expose the efficiency hint above the fold on results screen**

WHAT: Move the efficiency hint (currently computed at results.tsx line 481) from its current position (below XP bar, below boards, below share section) to a position immediately below the score display or adjacent to the worst-performing board. The hint already exists and already fires correctly when there is a meaningful swap. The only problem is placement. WHERE: results.tsx, restructure the scroll content order to surface the hint in the top half of the screen. Alternatively, surface it as a toast that fires 2 seconds after mount. WHY: The auto-continue countdown (AUTO_CONTINUE_SECS) means a significant fraction of players leave results before scrolling to the hint. A hint that exists but is never seen provides zero coaching value. EFFORT: S (layout change, no logic change). IMPACT: Increases the coaching loop completion rate — players who see "moving this card would have improved your result by 22%" learn from each game rather than repeating the same placement errors.

**Fix 5 — Add a streak-break grace period and set expectation in UI**

WHAT: Add a single-use streak shield mechanic: the streak persists through one missed day if the player has been on a streak of 7+ days. Display the shield status in the streak badge UI and in the daily reward modal. WHERE: The streak logic lives in the `claim_daily_streak` RPC (Supabase-side) and the client-side handler at index.tsx line 989. The badge is at index.tsx line 1353. WHY: The streak mechanic as currently implemented manufactures anxiety without providing a safety valve. A grace period turns the streak from a punishment mechanism into a loyalty reward. The player who breaks a 14-day streak due to travel or illness is the engaged player the app most needs to retain. Losing their streak is exactly the wrong moment to give them no mercy. The shield also gives the badge a new meaning: it becomes a safety indicator, not just a countdown clock. EFFORT: M (Supabase RPC change + client badge update). IMPACT: Reduces churn at streak-break moments, which are high-emotional-valence events and natural exit points.
